### 1.2 Does A$130 for 20 minutes fit the Perth range?

Short answer: the total dollar amount fits. The price per minute does not.

- **Total dollars: it fits.** A$130 sits between the A$120 and A$150 that Perth midwives and obstetricians charge for a later antenatal visit (A$120 Cherished, A$120 PWSC, A$130 POGS, A$150 My Midwives for 60 minutes).
- **Per minute: it is 2 to 3 times higher.** A$130 for 20 minutes is A$6.50 a minute. A$120 for 60 minutes is A$2.00 a minute. A$150 for 60 minutes is A$2.50 a minute. (My arithmetic.)
- **Out of pocket for the client: higher than the others.** With the A$32.95 rebate the client pays about A$97 net. At Cherished she pays about A$47 net for up to 60 minutes. At My Midwives she pays about A$74 for 60 minutes.
- **Why a GTT client will not mind:** she is not shopping for a midwife. She is buying a test and the consult is a required step before it (the referral and check). She compares the all-in price of the package, not the price per minute of the consult. This is my reading, not tested with real clients.
- **Why a standalone buyer will mind more:** a woman looking for a private consult only will compare us with a 60 minute visit at about A$120 to A$150. A 20 minute visit at A$130 will look expensive to her unless she is told clearly what it is (a short check, quick to book, near her day).

Rebate check: the page for item 82105 at https://privatehealth.gov.au/dynamic/MBSItems/PregnancyBirth/82105 confirms the item is a short antenatal visit by a participating midwife lasting at least 10 minutes. It did not show the dollar rebate, so I could not confirm A$32.95 myself. A$32.95 is a locked fact from the Round 20 brief. AskMBS should confirm it, as the brief already says for 73928.

### 1.3 Market read at A$130

A$130 sits inside the A$120 to A$150 band for later antenatal visits in total dollars. Per minute it is well above the A$2.00 to A$2.50 of a 60 minute midwife visit, so sell it as a short, fast, convenient check, not as a long midwife visit. For test clients the consult is small beside the full package price of A$320 (Package 1).

Suggested workaround so a standalone buyer does not feel short-changed: offer a longer standalone slot for those who want it (for example 40 minutes at a higher price). The Medicare item and rebate for a longer visit are NOT IN REPO and I have not checked them. This is listed as a question below.

### 2.2 Is refunding 50% of the lounge amount too generous?

Short answer: no. It is in line with the market, and arguably a little firm already.

Plain reading of the rule: the client attends her consult, then misses her test or is late. She gets 50% of the lounge amount back (50% of A$162.15 is about A$81.08, my sum) and pays the full lounge amount again to rebook. So GTT keeps 50% of the first booking as a fee. That is the same as a 50% late-cancel fee.

How it compares:
- Rates in the real policies I read: 50% (Beauty Studio inside 24 hours, Melbourne psychology), 100% (Perth IPC, Movement Clinic), flat A$50 (Appearance Clinic). A 50% fee sits in the middle.
- Where GTT is different: most of those businesses give a notice window first. GTT's rule as written in the brief only covers a miss or lateness on the day. It does not say what happens if the client calls 3 days ahead. I recommend adding that (below).
- What GTT actually loses: the chair, the lounge room and the staff for that 25 minute pair slot are already paid for and cannot be re-sold at short notice. The lab fee (A$27.85) goes straight to the lab and is not GTT's money. The consult was delivered and the Medicare rebate for it is already hers. This is my reasoning from the locked facts, not a calculated loss figure. A real loss figure per empty slot is NOT IN REPO.
- The main risk of being too tough is a client who is genuinely unwell. Pregnant clients can be sick or have a medical problem on the morning. A rigid 50% charge in that case is the exact thing the law guide warns about and would also be bad for the brand.

### 2.3 Recommended policy (plain English)

**Recommended: keep 50%, but add a notice window, a medical exception and a short grace time.**

1. **Free change with 24 hours notice or more.** The client can move her test with no charge. The lounge amount moves with her. (Matches Perth IPC, Beauty Studio, Movement Clinic, Appearance Clinic.)
2. **Less than 24 hours notice, no-show, or too late for the slot:** 50% of the lounge amount is refunded, and she pays the full lounge amount again on rebooking. This is the rule Anthony has set. Keep it.
3. **Grace time.** If she arrives up to a short grace time late and the slot can still be done, she keeps her booking with no fee. How long that grace time can be depends on the collection timing and the fasting window. I do not have that figure in the repo. See question 2.
4. **Medical exception.** If she cannot attend because of illness, a pregnancy problem, or an emergency, she can rebook once with no fee. The practice may ask for a message from a doctor or midwife, as Melbourne Child Psychology does. Work, travel or "forgot" do not count.
5. **Repeat misses.** After two misses she pays the lounge amount up front to rebook (the Movement Clinic does something similar).
6. **Say it clearly three times.** On the booking page, in the checkout, and in the confirmation message. Clear notice at booking is what makes a fee fair and enforceable.
7. **Her Medicare rebate for the consult is not affected**, because she attended and had the consult. Tell her this so she is not surprised.

**Alternative A: stricter and simpler.** No-show with no notice means no refund (100% kept). Late but within the slot means 50% kept. Easiest to explain and protects the day's roster best. Risk: feels harsh for a pregnant client, and more likely to cause complaints and chargebacks.

**Alternative B: gentler and flat.** Keep a flat A$50 instead of a percentage (like Appearance Clinic) and put the rest as credit for 60 days. Easier on the client and easy to say in one line. Risk: the A$50 is a guess at GTT's cost of a wasted slot, which is NOT IN REPO.

I recommend the Recommended policy. It keeps Anthony's 50% rule, matches the market, and adds the three things a law guide and the best real policy (Melbourne Child Psychology) show are needed: a notice window, a medical exception and a clear rebooking rule.

## 3. Demand for standalone private pregnancy consults

### 3.1 What real evidence exists

- **WA births:** about 30,516 births in WA in 2024, from a ScienceDirect paper found through search (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1871519226000880). I did not open the paper, so treat this as secondhand.
- **Models of care, 2023 (AIHW):** in Victoria, Queensland and WA combined, 45% of women used public hospital maternity care, 23% private obstetrician care, and 11% midwifery group practice care. In WA, 10% had the same midwife group through the whole pregnancy. Source: https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/mothers-babies/australias-mothers-babies/contents/maternity-models-of-care
- **Private midwives are few:** a directory page (https://vickihobbs.com/privately-practising-midwives-in-western-australia/, updated 11/08/2026, date format unclear) lists about 38 private midwives who attend births in WA (my count, 25 in Perth metro) and about 19 more who do antenatal and postnatal care only. It gives no number of women they see.
- **Hospital access is limited:** a search result said four WA maternity services give visiting rights to about 20 private midwives. Secondhand, not verified.
- **No WA data exists on how many women book a standalone private midwife consult.** The WA shared-care guidelines say shared care numbers are unknown in WA. AIHW gives no private midwife share. So any take-up number below is an assumption.

### 3.2 Take-up range (all ASSUMPTIONS)

Capacity first, from the locked facts:
- GTT consults need about 474 a month x 20 minutes, which is about 158 hours a month, or about 36 hours a week (my arithmetic from the Center of Truth volume).
- The Center of Truth shows 45 paid midwife hours a week. That roster was for 09:00 starts and may change for 08:00 to 16:00 starts. The new paid hours are NOT IN REPO.
- If 45 hours still holds, that leaves about 8 to 9 hours a week, which is about 25 standalone 20 minute slots a week. This is the ceiling for the scenarios.

| Scenario | Standalone consults a week (ASSUMPTION) | A year | Share of about 30,000 WA births (ASSUMPTION, treats each consult as a different woman) | Gross a week at A$130 (GST-free) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 5 | 260 | about 0.9% | A$650 |
| Middle | 12 | 624 | about 2.1% | A$1,560 |
| High | 25 (full capacity) | 1,300 | about 4.3% | A$3,250 |

Rough money check (ESTIMATE from locked rates, to be confirmed by Theodore):
- Midwife cost per paid hour is about A$45.06 blended times the 1.3658 on-cost, which is about A$61.54 an hour, or about A$20.51 per 20 minutes.
- Card fee at 1.7% plus A$0.30 on A$130 is about A$2.51.
- That leaves roughly A$107 per standalone consult before payroll tax and company tax. Because the midwives are already paid for the hours, the extra consults cost almost nothing extra, so the gain from filling time is close to the whole A$130 less card fee.
- Middle case: 12 a week x about A$107 is roughly A$1,280 a week before payroll tax and company tax, or about A$5,500 a month (x 4.33). This is a rough guide only. Payroll tax and tax were not run here.

Why I think low is the safest planning case (reasoning, not fact): a short 20 minute consult is an unusual product, midwives who already see these women offer longer visits at a lower price per minute, and the clinic has no existing client list at launch. The upside is that women who are not with a private midwife at all (about 45% in public care and 23% with obstetricians, from AIHW) could use a quick, cheap-in-time check.

Ways to lift take-up without new cost (workarounds): put the standalone consult on the same booking page as the test, offer it to women in the 1 to 28 day window who have not booked a test yet, and let partners and GPs know it exists once Anthony approves any outreach.
