## Finding 1: Health Insurance Act 1973 (Cth) s129AA — Prohibited Practices (Pathology Referral Inducements)

**Source (Tier 1):** [Federal Register of Legislation — Health Insurance Act 1973](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00101/2022-06-13); corroborated via [RCPA's own Prohibited Practices guideline](https://www.rcpa.edu.au/getattachment/ea84e3ca-0324-40da-bfa2-a104b3347a12/Prohibited-Practices.aspx) (Tier 2, the professional body for pathology).

**What the law says:** Section 129AA of the Health Insurance Act 1973 makes it an offence for a person who requests pathology services (a "requestor" — typically a GP, obstetrician, or other referring practitioner) to receive any property, benefit, or advantage from a person who provides pathology services, as an inducement connected to the referral, without reasonable excuse. The reverse also applies: a pathology-services provider (or someone acting on its behalf) offering such a benefit is equally an offence. "Inducement" is defined broadly — money, property, or any other benefit or advantage. The offence has been broadened over time to include bodies corporate, not just individuals.

**Why this is genuinely relevant to GTT Center Perth, not a generic caution:** this venture's own `docs/referral-partnership-plan.md` (already existing, not authored this session) describes a referral-relationship strategy built around:
- "Referral credit tracked via the booking system's 'How did you hear about us?' field"
- "Alternative: partner-specific pricing for their clients" (i.e., a preferential price offered specifically to a referring practice's clients)
- "Offer: complimentary wellness visits so the practice's own staff can experience the service firsthand"

**The genuinely open legal question, not resolved here:** GTT Center Perth is not itself the Approved Pathology Authority (WDP/PathWest/Clinipath/ACL holds that role) — it operates the collection venue and the surrounding wellness experience. Whether GTT Center Perth's referral-courtesy practices (complimentary visits for referring-practice staff, referral-tracking, any preferential client pricing tied to a specific referring practice) could be construed as connected to inducement for the referral of pathology services under s129AA — given GTT Center Perth's direct commercial and physical relationship to where those pathology services are collected — is a genuine, untested question this research cannot answer. It may well be that these practices are entirely standard, low-risk marketing courtesy (complimentary visits to build practitioner familiarity, not payment tied to referral volume) that fall well outside what s129AA targets (which is aimed squarely at direct financial inducements tied to referral volume, such as rent-free premises or cash payments to referrers). No prior reading of this repository's referral-partnership work identified this law at all — it is a genuinely new input, not a re-flagging of an existing concern.

**Recommendation, not a decision:** before referral-partnership outreach begins in earnest (currently gated on securing a venue address anyway, per `docs/referral-partnership-plan.md`'s own sequencing), have a solicitor review the referral-courtesy mechanics specifically against s129AA and the equivalent state-level inducement provisions, alongside the AHPRA Codes of Conduct's own conflict-of-interest provisions (which apply to the referring midwife/OB/GP as registered practitioners, not to GTT Center Perth directly, but which the referring practice would itself need to be comfortable with). This is a cheap, bounded piece of legal advice to obtain relative to the risk of an inadvertent breach once real referral relationships are operating. **Not itemised as an "everything must stop" blocker** — it is a professional-verification item to complete before the referral program moves from "planning" to "live outreach," consistent with how this repository already treats every other professional-verification item (e.g., insurance quotes, Fair Work Infoline confirmation).

## Finding 2: Prepaid-Package / Gift-Voucher Consumer Law and Non-Cash Payment Facility Exposure

**Source (Tier 1):** Australian Consumer Law gift-card provisions (NSW Government, WA Consumer Protection — see `OWNER-OPERATOR-FAILURE-INTELLIGENCE.md` Section 3 for full citations); general awareness that gift cards/vouchers/prepaid-value facilities can, depending on structure, fall within the Corporations Act's "non-cash payment facility" definition, which can require an Australian Financial Services Licence (AFSL) — this specific point is sourced from general secondary commentary found during this session's search and was not independently verified against ASIC's own regulatory guidance text; flagged as a genuinely open question, not a confirmed requirement.

**Why this is relevant to GTT Center Perth:** the locked commercial model collects the **full package price at the time of booking**, before the service is delivered (`docs/CURRENT-STATE.md` §2). This is economically similar to a prepaid voucher, even though it is sold as a direct booking rather than a separate "gift card" product. Two genuinely open questions, neither resolved here:
1. Does GTT Center Perth's own booking/payment structure (full payment at booking, for a specific dated appointment, not a transferable stored-value balance) fall inside or outside the "non-cash payment facility"/AFSL question at all? The Corporations Act framework is generally understood to target stored-value or transferable-balance facilities (classic gift cards) more than a direct pre-payment for one's own specific, dated appointment — but this venture's specific mechanics have not been checked against the actual regulatory text by a qualified person.
2. If GTT Center Perth ever introduces an actual gift-voucher product (mentioned as a feature in several competitor/analogue businesses researched in `COMPETITOR-ANALOGUE-DATABASE.md`, e.g. Yummy Mummy, Hidden Valley Eco) as a future marketing feature, the 3-year-minimum-expiry rule and the insurable/segregated-funds question (i.e., is prepaid client money held safely enough that a cash-flow crunch, of the exact kind evidenced in the real Adytum case, does not leave clients as unsecured creditors) should be built into that product's design from the start, not retrofitted.

**Recommendation, not a decision:** raise both questions with the accountant/solicitor already engaged for entity setup (per `docs/accountant-engagement-email-draft.md` and `docs/external-resources-and-advisors.md`) as part of the standard pre-launch professional review, rather than as a separate new engagement. This does not require changing the locked full-payment-at-booking model — it requires confirming that model's specific legal treatment, and, separately, planning proper handling of any future prepaid-package cash (e.g., not spending pre-collected client money on unrelated costs before the service is delivered, which is exactly the practice that harmed customers in the Adytum case).
