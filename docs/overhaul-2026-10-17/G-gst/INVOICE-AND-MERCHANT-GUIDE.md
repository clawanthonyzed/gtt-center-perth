## 1. Straight answer to "a correct split is a shield, not avoidance"

Half right. A split that reflects two real supplies is not avoidance: GSTR 2001/8 para 25 says "GST is payable on a mixed supply that you make, but only to the extent that the supply is taxable. You need to apportion the consideration for a mixed supply between the taxable and non-taxable parts". But the split is a consequence of the facts, not a tool to create them. The GST outcome follows what is supplied, by whom, and whether that supplier is within s 38-7 or s 38-10. GTT does not currently supply a GST-free service, so the correct figures for a GTT invoice are: 100% taxable. The honest split today is 0 / 100.

## 2. Itemised tax invoice rules (GST Act s 29-70)

A tax invoice must contain "enough information to enable the following to be clearly ascertained": (i) "the supplier's identity and the supplier's *ABN"; (iii) "what is supplied, including the quantity (if applicable) and the price of what is supplied"; (iv) "the extent to which each supply to which the document relates is a *taxable supply"; (v) "the date the document is issued"; (vi) "the amount of GST (if any) payable in relation to each supply". It must be in the approved form and show it is intended to be a tax invoice. The supplier must give one "within 28 days after the recipient of the supply requests it" (s 29-70(2)). The recipient's identity is required where the price is at least A$1,000 (s 29-70(1)(c)(ii)). Whether a lower threshold applies for issuing at all (the ATO's small-amount rule) was not read: TO CONFIRM with the accountant.

Practical rule: one line per genuinely separate supply, each with its own price, a clear "taxable" or "GST-free" marking, and the GST amount. Label a line GST-free only where the supplier is within s 38-7 or s 38-10 for that line.

## 3. Separate prices that match market value (GSTR 2001/8)

| Question | What GSTR 2001/8 says (copied) | What it means for GTT |
|---|---|---|
| When must you split? | Para 16: a mixed supply "has to be separated or unbundled as it contains separately identifiable taxable and non-taxable parts that need to be individually recognised" | Only if one part is truly GST-free |
| When do you not split? | Para 17: a composite supply "contains a dominant part and includes something that is integral, ancillary or incidental to that part. You treat a composite supply as a supply of a single thing." Para 18A: "If a composite supply is taxable, then GST is payable on the whole supply" | The lounge, water, tea and tablet around two massages are incidental to the wellness treatments: one taxable supply, whatever the invoice says |
| The tests | Para 59: indicators that a part is integral, ancillary or incidental: "a means of better enjoying the dominant thing supplied, rather than constituting for customers an aim in itself"; "a marginal proportion of the total value of the package"; "necessary or contributes to the supply as a whole, but cannot be identified as the dominant part"; "contributes to the proper performance of the contract". Para 59A: "It is a question of fact and degree" | Applied to the day: if a client buys the visit for the wellness treatments, the lounge is incidental; if a client buys a real clinical service she would buy anyway, it is a separate part |
| The 20% or A$3 safe harbour | Para 21 and 60: you "may treat something ... as being integral, ancillary or incidental if the consideration that would be apportioned to it ... does not exceed the lesser of $3.00; or 20% of the consideration for the total supply" | Only helps to treat a small part as incidental. It does not let a large part be re-labelled |
| Invoice labels do not decide it | Para 57 (quoting a UK case adopted in the ruling): "the fact that separate charges are identified in a contract or on an invoice does not on a consideration of all the circumstances necessarily prevent all the supplies from constituting one composite transaction" | A line called "clinical component" proves nothing |
| How to apportion | Para 26: "Apportionment must be undertaken as a matter of practical commonsense. You can use any reasonable basis". Para 27: "You should keep records that explain the basis used". Para 92: "the apportionment must be supportable by the facts in the particular circumstances" | Keep the workings |
| Preferred method | Para 97: direct methods "usually give you the most accurate measure", including "the price allocation as agreed between the parties" and "the comparative price of each part if it were supplied on its own". Para 98: "where it is possible to determine the price for which each part would have been supplied if it was supplied separately ... an apportionment on this basis may be reasonable" | Publish a separate stand-alone price for every component and use those |
| Indirect methods | Para 109: "a reasonable method that includes the addition of an appropriate mark-up". Para 112: "it may not be reasonable to apportion the consideration solely on the basis of the cost of the supply to you. This would be the case if your mark-up varies from part to part." Para 113: historical cost and residual value "may not reflect an appropriate apportionment" | Cost-only splits (for example the phlebotomist's wages) are weak unless the same margin applies to every component |
| Contrived allocation | Para 81ZD: apportioning a discount wholly to the taxable component "is merely a contrivance to reduce the GST otherwise payable, and does not reflect the true commercial position, that would not be a practical, commonsense basis". Para 95: the method should be chosen "not because it gives you a particular result". Para 96: apportionment that "cannot be justified in terms of reasonableness" may engage the general anti-avoidance provisions. Para 81ZI: a doctor who charged only for the GST-free drug and nothing for the taxable procedure made "a mixed supply and the consideration is in respect of both parts. It is unreasonable ... There is no commercial reason ... to not apply any of the consideration" | A tiny price on the lounge and wellness, with a big price on a "clinical" line, is the ATO's own example of what fails |

## 4. What Division 165 and Part IVA look at

Division 165 (the GST anti-avoidance rule), copied from the Act:
- Purpose: "The object of this Division is to deter schemes to give entities benefits by reducing GST, increasing refunds or altering the timing of payment of GST or refunds." It "is aimed at artificial or contrived schemes."
- Operation (s 165-5(1)): a "GST benefit" from a "scheme"; the benefit is not from a choice expressly provided by the GST law; and "it is reasonable to conclude that" either an entity entered into or carried out the scheme "with the sole or dominant purpose" of a GST benefit, or "the principal effect of the scheme ... is that the avoider gets the GST benefit".
- "Scheme" (s 165-10(2)) is wide: "any arrangement, agreement, understanding, promise or undertaking ... whether or not it is, or is intended to be, enforceable" and "any scheme, plan, proposal, action, course of action or course of conduct".
- Matters considered (s 165-15): "the manner in which the scheme was entered into or carried out; the form and substance of the scheme, including the legal rights and obligations involved and the economic and commercial substance"; the purpose or object of the Act; timing; "any change in the avoider's financial position that has resulted ... from the scheme"; whether dealings are at arm's length.
- Remedy (s 165-40 and 165-55): the Commissioner may declare the amount of GST that should have been payable and may "treat a particular event that actually happened as not having happened" and the reverse.

Part IVA (Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s 177D) is titled "Schemes to reduce income tax". It applies where a person entered into or carried out a scheme "for the purpose of ... enabling a taxpayer ... to obtain a tax benefit", having regard to "the manner in which the scheme was entered into or carried out; the form and substance of the scheme; the time ...; the result ... that, but for this Part, would be achieved by the scheme; any change in the financial position of the relevant taxpayer ...". It is an income tax rule. For a GST split it matters only if the structure also moves taxable profit between entities.

Reading these together for an A/B split: the question will be (1) is there a real supply behind Component A, supplied by this supplier; (2) is the price of each part a fair market measure; (3) would the arrangement make commercial sense if there were no GST saving. A split priced to the tax saving, with no GST-free supply behind it, fails all three.

## 5. Merchant payments and bank accounts

- GST attaches to supplies, not to accounts. A taxable supply is made when "you make the supply for consideration" in the course of an enterprise you carry on (s 9-5). Consideration includes "any payment ... in connection with a supply" (s 9-15). Which bank account or merchant ID receives the money does not change whether the supply is taxable or GST-free.
- One merchant account with itemised receipts is the clean model. Every card payment settles to the entity that made the supply, and the receipt and tax invoice show each line with its GST.
- Separate merchant accounts or entities do not change the GST outcome unless the second entity genuinely makes the supply. A separate entity for "Component A" helps only if that entity really employs the qualified person, bears the responsibility, bills in its own name and is recognised for the relevant service (for example a registered nurse employed by that entity providing a nursing service under s 38-10, or a lab-run collection under s 38-7). If the entity merely receives a share of the client's payment for something GTT delivers, that is a scheme within s 165-10(2) and the allocation is exposed to s 165-5.
- Pathology fees must not pass through GTT's merchant account. If a client pays a private lab fee, the lab collects it. The Medicare benefit is paid to the lab. GTT never holds or splits the lab's money (STATUTE-ANALYSIS.md S2).
- Prepayments and refunds: GTT's rule is full prepayment at booking. GST on a prepayment arises when consideration is received or an invoice is issued, whichever is earlier (rule not re-read this round: TO CONFIRM with the accountant). Refunds need an adjustment note.

## 7. What the ATO private ruling should ask (draft questions only)

ATO guidance read: the application must give "a description of all the facts relevant to your scheme or circumstance", "Your private ruling won't apply if there is any substantial difference between what you" describe and what happens, and the ATO may decline where "you're not seriously contemplating the arrangement you describe". So ask only about the one model GTT actually adopts. Draft questions:

1. Is a [named] service by [AHPRA-registered nurse / APD dietitian / physiotherapist], supplied by [entity] to an expecting mother, a supply that is GST-free under s 38-10(1)?
2. If a registered nurse bills a service and a phlebotomist assists under the nurse's direct supervision, are the assistant's services included under the ATO assistant conditions (billed in the nurse's name, full responsibility, direct supervision)? What does "each treatment" require?
3. Where the service is sold with taxable wellness services at a package price, is the apportionment by stand-alone prices (GSTR 2001/8 para 98) a reasonable basis under s 9-80?
4. Does the lounge form part of one composite taxable supply with the wellness treatments (GSTR 2001/8 paras 17 and 59)?
5. Would Division 165 apply to the arrangement as described?
6. Is a collection or room fee paid by the pathology partner to the entity a taxable supply at 10%, and is any part GST-free?

Facts to describe: entity, ABN, who delivers each service and their registration, the price list, the pathology partner agreement showing the lab bills and is responsible, the invoice and receipt templates. Do not lodge until the model and entity are settled (E workstream) and the accountant has reviewed. ## 8. Honest conclusion: where the line is

| Ratio or approach | Defensible? | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| 0 / 100 (all taxable) | Yes. Correct today | None. Keep the fully taxable base and claim credits |
| Real GST-free allied-health or nursing line priced at its own stand-alone price, delivered by a recognised professional | Yes, for that line only | Registration, separate price list, role description, ruling for any nurse or assistant structure |
| Lab-run collection in GTT's rooms; lab pays GTT a market fee (taxable) | Yes | Written lab agreement, market fee evidence (no quote yet) |
| 50 / 50, 60 / 40, 70 / 30 on the A$250 with no GTT GST-free supply behind Component A | No | Cannot be supported. The whole Medicare lab fee for the visit is A$25.85. A$125 to A$175 is 4.8 to 6.8 times that, and 4.1 to 5.7 times GTT's own collection cost of A$30.73 a client. The ATO would test a price that high against the lab's fee schedule and cost |
| Value split at market anchor (A$25.85 or A$5.95) | Only as arithmetic. It values the lab's service, which is billed to Medicare, so it is not GTT revenue | Would need a GTT supply of that service |
| Cost share (20.13%) | Weak | Para 112: only reasonable if mark-ups are the same across parts; wellness margins and collection margins differ |
| 100 / 0 | No | Treats the lounge and wellness as worth nothing: para 81ZD and 81ZI |

Line between a correct split and avoidance, in one sentence: it is correct when each line is a real supply, by a supplier within the statute for that line, at a price an independent buyer would see on a price list; it is avoidance when the label or the bank account is chosen to produce the GST result and the substance is unchanged.
