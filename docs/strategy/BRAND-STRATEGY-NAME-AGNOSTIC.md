# Brand Strategy — Name-Agnostic

**Status:** Current. Written to work identically for SOLENA or ELOWEN (the two live candidates — see `docs/naming/NAMING-DECISION-STATE.md` for current naming status; ELOWYN is documented, not pursued further absent new information). Nothing here should require rewriting once the name locks — only the wordmark, palette, and type system are name-dependent, and none of those are decided here.

**Supersedes, in part:** `docs/brand-guide.md`'s positioning section (§1) — that document's four pillars (clinical confidence, premium warmth, maternal knowing, calm authority) were built around "GTT Center Perth" as a descriptive clinical name and around reassurance as the primary brand job. This document keeps what still works from it (the tone-of-voice rules, the personality sketch, the "we are not a day spa pretending to be a clinic" discipline) and revises the emphasis per the founder's explicit instruction: the brand should not be optimised solely around reassurance/clinical trust — the voluntary repeat visit, which is desire-driven, is the economically important interaction. `docs/brand-guide.md` itself remains un-edited and is superseded by this document specifically on positioning emphasis, not on tone mechanics.

---

## Brand Purpose

To make the hours a woman is required to spend on a mandatory pregnancy test the best hours of her week — and to become, from that first visit, a place she keeps choosing long after the test is behind her.

## Brand Promise

However she arrives — nervous, fasting, referred, unfamiliar with the building — she leaves cared for, and she wants to come back on a day she didn't have to.

## Brand Essence

*Care, without asking to be endured.*

## Mission

Build Perth's first venue that treats the GTT wait as the product, not the obstacle — and grow it into a destination Perth women choose for themselves, independent of any referral.

## Vision

To be the premium women's house Perth women think of first — for the test they have to take, and for every appointment after it they didn't have to.

## Values

- **Competence first, warmth always.** A real clinical process happens here; nothing about the hospitality layer is allowed to undermine confidence in it.
- **Restraint over performance.** Care that is beautifully organised, not care that announces itself. No forced cheerfulness, no scripted warmth.
- **Every woman, not just every pregnant woman.** The afternoon business exists on its own terms — nothing in the experience should make a non-pregnant client feel like a guest in someone else's clinic.
- **Desire earns the second visit; trust earns the first.** Both matter. Neither substitutes for the other.
- **Say what you mean, briefly.** No clinical jargon, no wellness cliché, no forced luxury language.

## Target Customer

**Primary (AM):** A woman referred for a GTT at 24–28 weeks' gestation. She did not choose this appointment — her midwife or OB did. She has been fasting since the night before. She has, at minimum, some anxiety about the needle, the result, or both, layered on the general uncertainty of pregnancy.

**Primary (PM/standalone):** Any woman booking massage, nails, brows, or hair — pregnant or not, GTT client or not. She is choosing this appointment for herself, the same way she'd choose any premium beauty/wellness booking, and has zero patience for anything that reads as medical, maternal, or clinical.

**What she wants:** to feel like a person, not a case; to spend an unavoidable block of her week somewhere that makes it worth having spent; to trust that the clinical part is being handled properly without having to manage that herself.

**What she fears:** a fluorescent-lit waiting room; being spoken to like a patient rather than an adult; a needle handled badly; being made to feel her body or her pregnancy is on public display; wasting money on something that looks nicer than it feels.

**What frustrates her about conventional healthcare:** being processed rather than attended to; being told to wait with no sense of when or why; forms and reference numbers instead of a name; environments designed for throughput, not for her.

**What makes her feel cared for:** being expected by name; a physical environment that doesn't feel clinical even where clinical rigour is genuinely present; small, specific gestures (a real breakfast, not a vending machine; a note that anticipates a question before she asks it); staff who don't perform hospitality at her.

**What makes her spend:** confidence that the extra cost buys something real — time, comfort, privacy, a genuinely good treatment — not just nicer furniture around the same wait.

**What makes her return:** the second visit has to be chosen, not owed. She returns because the massage was good, the room was calm, and nobody made her feel like a patient on the way out.

**What makes her recommend a place:** an experience specific enough to describe in one sentence to a friend ("they gave me breakfast and did my nails after my last blood draw" is a sentence people actually say — corrected 2026-08-16, see changelog: the client is fasting throughout the GTT and never receives results at the venue, so the food/treatment happen after her final draw, not "while waiting for results") — genericness doesn't travel by word of mouth, specificity does.

**What signals luxury to her:** restraint, quiet materials, staff who are calm rather than eager, an environment that doesn't need to explain itself.

**What signals "cheap" or "clinical" to her:** fluorescent downlights, laminate counters, forms on a clipboard, being called by a number or a diagnosis, visible medical equipment outside the one room that genuinely needs it.

## Emotional Benefit

Feeling looked after, without feeling managed.

## Functional Benefit

A mandatory ~2.5-hour medical appointment is converted into productive, enjoyable time — collection happens on schedule, and the wait is filled with a real service instead of a waiting room.

## Reasons to Believe

- Real clinical credibility: phlebotomy delivered by employed, credentialed staff operating under a NATA-accredited pathology partner's umbrella — not a wellness business improvising a medical service.
- A genuinely full service menu (massage, nails, hair, beauty) in one venue, not a single treatment bolted onto a waiting room.
- No direct GTT-integrated competitor in WA (`docs/business-plan.md` §7) — first-mover credibility in this specific category, regionally.
- An afternoon business that stands on its own, proving the brand isn't just "the GTT place" — a woman can book a Thursday facial having never had a GTT here at all.

## Differentiators

Multi-client concurrent model (not a single-client-at-a-time boutique); full-service breadth in one venue; genuine clinical rigour under real accreditation, not a lifestyle brand playing at healthcare; an explicit design principle (per both Phase 1/Phase 2 design exploration) that the wait itself is the product, not an inconvenience to be minimised.

## Brand Personality

Carried forward from `docs/brand-guide.md`'s personality sketch, because it tested well and is name-independent: **a woman in her mid-30s with excellent taste and no interest in performing it — professional but not corporate, warm but not gushing, knows a great deal and doesn't need to prove it, remembers your name.** Both the Elowen and Solena design explorations arrived at compatible variations of this same person (Elowen: "considered, knowing, unhurried"; Solena: "warm, generous, sensory") — this is the shared spine underneath either name, not two different people.

## Brand Voice

- Complete sentences, plain English, active voice.
- No clinical jargon ("patient," "reference number," "results") in customer-facing copy — a first name and a plain description instead.
- No forced luxury language, no "journey," no exclamation marks in clinical contexts, one emoji maximum anywhere.
- Reassurance where it's needed (fasting instructions, the morning of), invitation where it isn't (afternoon bookings, service menus) — the same brand, two registers, not two voices.
- Direct inheritance from `docs/brand-guide.md`'s existing voice rules (avoid "amazing," "incredible," "game-changer," "journey," "empowered") — this discipline is retained in full, it was already correct.

## Experience Principles

1. **The clinical process is real and should look it, exactly once** — one room in the venue is allowed to look clinical, because it is; everywhere else earns the right to feel like a private house precisely because that one room is handled properly.
2. **Nothing that happens should require her to manage it herself** — no clipboards, no "let us know if you need anything," proactive rather than reactive care.
3. **The wait is scheduled, not endured** — every minute between draws has a named purpose (a treatment, breakfast, a moment of nothing), never unstructured dead time.
4. **The afternoon customer must never feel like she's in someone else's clinic** — no pregnancy-coded material visible to a non-pregnant guest; the standalone business has to work as a standalone business, in the room, not just on paper.
5. **Leave her with something specific to remember and repeat** — a physical object, a sentence, a gesture worth describing to a friend, not a generic "thanks for coming."

## Referral Strategy

Word-of-mouth is the primary, lowest-cost acquisition channel for both the referral pathway (midwife/OB) and peer recommendation (friend-to-friend) — which is precisely why naming decisions have weighed referral clarity/spelling ease as a first-order criterion, not an afterthought (`docs/naming/NAMING-FINAL-COMPARISON.md` §3). Operationally: staff should be trained to give referring midwives/OBs a specific, easy-to-repeat description of what happens here, not a generic "wellness experience" pitch — clinicians refer to things they can describe precisely to a patient in thirty seconds.

## Hospitality Strategy

Borrowed deliberately from hospitality, not healthcare: guests are expected, greeted by name, and handed a physical itinerary rather than told to wait and watch a screen (`docs/experience/CUSTOMER-JOURNEY.md`'s "welcome card" concept, present in both design explorations independently — a strong, name-independent signal it's the right idea regardless of which name wins). Staff uniform, language, and pacing should read as considered hospitality staff, not clinical staff in soft clothing.
