# Premium Positioning & Cost Discipline

**Status:** Current, name-agnostic. Companion to `docs/strategy/BRAND-STRATEGY-NAME-AGNOSTIC.md`. Answers two linked questions: what actually creates premium perception here, and how should spend be prioritised before a name/venue is locked.

---

## The Target Position

**Premium, not luxury.** `docs/brand-guide.md` §11 already established this distinction correctly and it's retained here unchanged: "premium" not "luxury," so as not to alienate the entry-price customer. The founder's brief for this phase raises the bar slightly ("premium luxury+") — read here as *premium executed without compromise*, not as a shift into exclusivity signalling. The business still needs the A$80 entry package customer to feel she belongs.

**Explicitly not:** budget, mass-market, generic clinic, ordinary beauty salon, generic day spa, boho, cliché-luxury (excess gold/marble), or trend-led design that will date within a few years — all directly named as failure modes in prior work and reconfirmed here.

## What Actually Creates Premium Perception (Evidence-Based, Not Assumed)

Both Phase 1 and Phase 2 design explorations, working independently and reaching different naming conclusions, arrived at the **same** answer to this question — which makes it unusually reliable as a shared finding rather than a single team's opinion:

1. **Restraint over decoration.** Fewer, better choices — one accent colour used sparingly, not several competing for attention; typography with generous space around it, not dense or ornamented.
2. **Light.** Natural light, or convincingly warm artificial light, does more perceived-luxury work per dollar than almost any other single intervention — both boards independently made this the centrepiece of their cheaper-to-execute recommendations.
3. **Material consistency, not material excess.** One or two genuinely good material decisions (a real oak table, a real plaster finish) executed properly and repeated consistently outperforms many mediocre-to-good materials used inconsistently.
4. **Service and pacing.** An unhurried, expected, name-known welcome reads as more premium than an expensively finished room with a transactional front desk.
5. **The absence of clinical signalling** outside the one room that needs it — the single highest-leverage "premium vs clinical" lever in the whole business, and one of the cheapest to control (it's a design/operations discipline, not a spend).

**What does not reliably create premium perception, evidenced by both boards' own explicit warnings:** expensive materials used broadly rather than selectively (the named "failure mode" of an underfunded restrained identity reading as merely plain, not restrained); decorative complexity (excess gold, marble, arches, botanical illustration); multiple competing typefaces or visual devices; anything that reads as trend-driven rather than considered.

## Cost-Discipline Classification

Every physical/design/experience recommendation surfaced across this project's work to date, classified. This list will grow as venue-specific decisions are made — treat it as a living reference, not a closed set.

### HIGH IMPACT / LOW COST
- Natural light prioritisation in room/venue selection and layout (a site-selection criterion, not a spend)
- The welcome/itinerary card (both boards costed this at roughly $1–2 per card, called "the single most important brand object in the business")
- The leaving box/gesture (costed at roughly $9–10 per guest in the design work reviewed — food plus one small item)
- Removing clinical signalling from guest-facing areas (a discipline, not a purchase)
- First-name-based reception model, no clipboard/counter intake process (an operations decision, not a spend)
- Staff continuity policy supporting the return loop (`docs/experience/RETURN-LOOP.md`) — an HR/rostering decision, largely cost-neutral against current staffing

### HIGH IMPACT / MEDIUM COST
- One genuinely good, consistently-used material choice for guest-facing surfaces (a real table, a real plaster or timber finish in the areas guests actually touch/see closely)
- Professional documentary-style photography once the venue exists — both boards note a single well-shot day produces a year of usable content, and that the room matters more to the result than the shoot budget
- Considered signage (brass/engraved letterforms) over standard vinyl/lightbox signage — a genuine cost premium, but named directly by both boards as the correct trade against a "generic salon strip" outcome

### HIGH IMPACT / HIGH COST
- Full-room material execution to the standard either design exploration envisions (oiled oak joinery, polished plaster at scale, etc.) — real money, and both boards agree this is where underfunding is most visible and most damaging to premium perception specifically
- A fully custom, non-stock photography library covering every service category

### LOW IMPACT / LOW COST
- Small decorative details with no clear connection to the brand's actual differentiators (default stock plants, generic wellness signage phrases) — permissible but not worth planning around
- Standard hashtag/caption conventions for social — necessary hygiene, not a differentiator

### LOW IMPACT / HIGH COST
- A fully bespoke symbol/logo device where a wordmark-led identity would perform equivalently (both boards independently lean wordmark-first, symbol-optional — see `docs/naming/NAMING-FINAL-COMPARISON.md` for the specific mark recommendations per candidate)
- Elaborate uniforms or packaging systems before the venue itself is proven — sequencing risk more than a pure cost-effectiveness problem, flagged here because it recurs as a temptation in early-stage brand work

## Prioritisation Rule for This Phase

Fund every HIGH IMPACT / LOW COST item without hesitation — they are cheap enough that "which name wins" doesn't change the calculus, and several are pure discipline rather than spend. Treat HIGH IMPACT / MEDIUM COST items as the first real budget conversation once a venue is confirmed. Defer HIGH IMPACT / HIGH COST items until the name is locked and the venue is real — spending here on a not-yet-final identity is the exact premature-production-lock the founder's brief explicitly warns against (`docs/naming/NAMING-DECISION-STATE.md`'s "DO NOT START YET" list).

## Maximum Perceived Luxury Per Dollar — Applied

The recurring instruction across this project's design work is a genuine, testable principle, not a slogan: at every spend decision, ask whether the money is buying **restraint executed well** or **decoration used to compensate for a lack of restraint.** The former is premium; the latter reads as trying, which is the opposite of premium regardless of the dollar figure spent.
